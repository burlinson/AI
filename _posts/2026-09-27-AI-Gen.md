Across the corporate world, [graduate hiring plans are changing](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine/): [Reduce the graduate intake](https://fortune.com/2025/08/15/ai-gutting-next-generation-of-talent/), because AI will be able to do the grunt work. That's a huge bet. It's also a bet that cuts against the grain of history.

The corporate world is currently littered with [AI initiatives that have not been able to progress beyond POCs](https://www.theregister.com/2025/08/18/generative_ai_zero_return_95_percent). Additionally, corporate AI use is still in its infancy.

If AI follows the patterns of previous technology waves, then we don't yet know how AI is going to unfold and where the true value proposition lies.

When the internet started, everyone was fixated on the technical aspects of connecting to the internet. Firms like [AOL](https://www.npr.org/2010/05/24/127045793/aols-journey-from-dial-up-to-a-new-brand) and [Excite](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001020620/000089161899000706/0000891618-99-000706.txt/seq-5) were valued at crazy prices. All they essentially did was connect us to rudimentary web pages via [land-based phone lines](https://www.npr.org/2025/08/12/nx-s1-5499539/aol-dial-up-ending).

It was only much later as the internet matured that firms like [Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/the-history-of-salesforce/), [Facebook](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/february-4/facebook-launches-mark-zuckerberg), [Airbnb](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/airbnb), [Apple](https://www.apple.com/), and [Uber](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Uber) appeared, creating entirely new products that were built on top of internet technologies.

At the beginning of the internet wave we could not have imagined some of the products and services that are now part of our everyday life.

AI likely has the same wondrous surprises for us in the coming decade.

At first glance, the logic for firms to cull workforces is seductive.

AI can summarize a contract, reconcile data and create spiffy looking decks in seconds. Why pay a 22-year-old to do it slowly, and at a much higher cost?

The financial saving shows up this quarter. The loss of shareholder value arrives a decade later.

**Judgment isn't taught in a classroom. It is built by doing tedious work badly, then less badly, and eventually well.**

The junior analyst reconciling a hundred breaks learns what a real error looks like. Over time the analyst's real-world learning develops into something more profound.

They learn that being 99% accurate isn't good enough. The best analysts are 100% certain about the accuracy of every aspect of their work product. At that point, the best analysts discover what it's like to have the respect and trust of senior leaders.

A junior analyst learns when to escalate to their boss and when to just fix the problem themselves. A great analyst knows that when they take a problem to their boss, they need to come armed with a potential solution.

The boring repetitive grunt work was never the point.

It was the apprenticeship. The fast learners who have strong emotional intelligence progress on to become the next generation of leaders.

Firms that reduce their investment in developing junior graduates won't notice at first, because their senior leaders are still in the building.

In the 1990s, firms [flattened their organizational hierarchies](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jomstd/v40y2003i2p225-256.html) to make quicker decisions and outsourced "low-value" tasks to focus their internal talent on high impact activities.

The cost savings looked brilliant on paper.

It was only years later that many firms discovered that no one understood who was accountable for execution and delivery. Moreover, the people left in the building [had lost sight of how the business really worked](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/6b2de70d-1d6f-48d4-8a47-9405ac11c1a5/content) or how products got delivered to their clients. That gritty grunt work had been outsourced to low-cost centers.

There is an even deeper irony to the culling of junior roles in the AI age.

AI is probabilistic. It sounds just as confident when it is right as when it is totally wrong. The safeguard is a named human who can tell the difference between an accurate answer and when AI is hallucinating.

That innate skill comes only from experience.

If there aren't any juniors who understand the detailed grunt work, who will be qualified to sign off on AI's streamlined version?

There is a second reason the bet on junior resourcing could be wrong.

**For hundreds of years, every new technology wave has kicked off with [dire predictions of workforce reductions](https://www.history.com/news/who-were-the-luddites).**

That's rarely how it plays out.

In 1995 no one was hiring social media managers, web developers or data scientists.

A decade from now today's graduates will be working in [roles nobody can even begin to describe today](https://news.mit.edu/2024/most-work-is-new-work-us-census-data-shows-0401). Cutting the graduate intake not only diminishes the pool of future leaders, it also significantly reduces a firm's ability to deliver new AI-driven products that don't yet exist.

The [ATM was supposed to kill the bank teller](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2016/09/why-automation-doesnt-mean-a-robot-is-going-to-take-your-job/). Instead, cheaper branches meant more branches. [Tellers upskilled from counting cash to filling customer relationship roles](https://www.atmmarketplace.com/articles/tellers-technology-and-atms/). Technology eliminated one role, but the expansion of branches demanded another much more sophisticated role.

[The spreadsheet eliminated the bookkeeper role, but then the number of accountants grew.](https://www.npr.org/sections/money/2015/02/25/389027988/episode-606-spreadsheets) Financial analysis became so cheap that firms could perform deeper analysis and run their business more efficiently.

When an automated client proposal or deeper insights into a client relationship costs almost nothing, firms won't do the same work with fewer people. They'll start working on better products and services that they simply didn't have the capacity to do before. When one firm provides superior products and services, all the others will need to follow to remain competitive.

When AI can produce answers in seconds, the most valuable employee will be the person who knows the right question to ask in the first place. Even more valuable is the person with experience to know when AI's answer just doesn't feel right.

From the late 1990s and well into the following two decades, firms [could not hire technology talent fast enough](https://ssti.org/node/10706). [Universities struggled to keep up with the supply of computer literate graduates](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/eroberts/CSCapacity/CSCapacity-History2000s.html), because nobody was sure what needed to be on the curriculum.

AI will be no different.

The graduates who learn AI on the job will be the ones who indirectly write AI curricula for universities. Firms that hire graduates to be their future AI leaders will likely be the same firms that will invent the new AI products that no one can yet imagine.

**By 2035, some of the most critical roles in corporate America will be held by people with titles that no one can describe today. Many of them are in university right now, studying subjects with no obvious connection to AI. Yet most will go on to build amazing careers in AI.**
