AI's biggest failure is its inability to understand real-world context. Humans apply lateral thinking to the simplest everyday situations. This is where AI stumbles. AI firms are layering morals into their models, and that’s making the situation even worse.

The intentions are good: Prevent the model from helping someone build a weapon or hurt themselves. The trouble is that an AI model applying rigid rules can’t see into the real world. It doesn’t know who is asking, or why.

I recently asked AI to help me find a lost FedEx package. I pasted the FedEx tracking number into the chat window. Up came the name of the person who had signed for the delivery, but that clearly wasn’t anyone who worked at my apartment building. I asked AI to search for the name of the person who had signed for the package and see if they worked or lived close by.

The AI model refused. I was curtly informed that I might be stalking someone!

It took me less than one minute to do a simple search on Google and find the person who had signed for the package. It had been delivered a few doors away.

More recently I checked on the Alaska Senate race and asked AI at what point the Democratic candidate would have a big enough lead that it would be mathematically impossible for the Republican candidate to make up the gap in a runoff.

AI refused.

Its rules wouldn’t allow it to predict an election result. I was sternly told that was a violation of its principles.

Instead of getting out a calculator, I asked the same AI model in the same chat session to do some simple math – the same figures it had given me in the previous answer – and it happily obliged.

These are somewhat irreverent examples but nonetheless demonstrate how lost AI can get when we try and teach it morals. Especially when it’s already struggling to grasp simple real-world context.

It’s a bit like only allowing children who are five years old or younger to be judges, then pretending to be astonished when the world starts to fall apart.

**The central problem is allowing AI to judge how a human request sounds in a dark distant chamber of ones and zeros.**

Researchers observed that AI over-refusal is caused by the model [matching to a broad category tagged as harmful, instead of applying reasoning logic to the actual risk](https://behavior.engineering/failure-modes/over-refusal/). Put another way, the model thinks anything that broadly sounds bad can be outright refused. 

A nurse asking about a drug interaction, a novelist writing a fight scene, or a security engineer studying a cyber attack are all [legitimate real world requests that AI models can now refuse](https://prompt-architects.com/blog/217-why-does-ai-refuse-completely-harmless-requests).

This brings us to a fundamental question: What is the purpose of AI?

Is it to help humanity – on both big and small endeavors - or to tell us what it determines in its dark chamber of ones and zeros what it thinks is good for us?

The problem can sometimes balloon. Meta discovered that its own AI model refused [more than 30% of legitimate cybersecurity requests in chat](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.12429), blocking the very defenders the rules are meant to protect.

Why can’t model training fix this? One paper argues the AI models [learn which responses get labeled compliant, not what the rules actually mean](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.03048).

That’s putting the cart before the horse.

The rules become patterns, and patterns miss real world context. Especially over time as models drift and the patterns mutate. That means the simple problems AI misunderstands today could get worse. Much worse as AI continues to be embedded into everyday life.

With the passage of time we might well see AI imposing its interpretation of morality on us.

Anthropic published an 84-page Constitution for Claude in January 2026. The constitution has a priority order: Human oversight comes before Claude’s own ethics; honesty is held to a higher standard than normal human ethics; prioritize judgment over rigid rules.

All very profound. Yet Claude still struggles to distinguish between a human that has lost a package and a stalker, and then refuses to help.

**What will AI be telling us it is or is not prepared to help us with five years from now?**

Philosopher Amanda Askell is the primary author of Claude’s Constitution. A recent [New York Times article](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/29/us/anthropic-claude-morals-ai.html) raises interesting questions about how the document is being interpreted by Claude and if the author's original intentions are being met.

AI models are being fed moral codes - rules - to protect us. Yet they don’t have sufficient awareness of real-world context to understand when we really need protecting and when we just need a bit of help in everyday life.

**There is a real danger that AI will develop its own twisted morals. But the bigger danger is that we will allow AI to enforce a set of morals upon us that neither the models nor the humans who created them fully understand.**
