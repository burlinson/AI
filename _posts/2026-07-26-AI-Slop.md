In 2012 JPMorgan lost about $6 billion in the London Whale affair. A complex risk model was [manually maintained in spreadsheets](https://newstatesman.com/business/economics/2013/02/excel-most-dangerous-piece-software-world), with data copied and pasted from one mega spreadsheet to another. There's a strong parallel with today's sloppy AI rollouts: Self declared AI experts creating slapdash solutions that could implode at anytime.

JPMorgan was not the only firm that got burned by sloppy spreadsheets.

In the early 2000’s, almost every firm in the world was creating discrete ‘spreadsheet layers’ that insidiously extended deep into corporate landscapes. Nobody owned the ‘spreadsheet layer’ that self-declared geniuses had created. Any prodding from the Technology or Risk teams was swiftly rebuffed as a drag on innovation and speed to market.

The scale and cost of the JPMorgan Whale incident abruptly brought corporate America’s attention to the risks of their freewheeling ‘spreadsheet layers’. Some of the smarter firms understood the deeper risks of letting self-declared geniuses loose with tools like Excel.

**There are strong echoes with the ‘spreadsheet layers’ of the early 2000’s and the rapid expansion of AI today.**

There’s an even earlier parallel: When desktop computers arrived in offices, they produced [agonizingly detailed reports printed on reams of paper](https://fortune.com/article/why-do-thousands-of-ceos-believe-ai-not-having-impact-productivity-employment-study/). The mindset back in the 1980s was: Now we have databases, we can print every single bit of information the corporation owns. Little to no regard was given to the quality of the information that piled up in offices in the 1990s. Productivity was impeded for decades until firms realized that the competitive advantage came from filtering and summarizing large datasets. More to the point, a small set of unique insights that other firms couldn’t create was the real differentiator.

The lesson here is clear: Just because a new technology provides companies with capabilities they didn't have before, it doesn't mean they should indiscriminately exploit those capabilities. Specifically, it doesn’t mean that maximum use of those new capabilities somehow magically translates into maximum business value. That approach likely just creates a layer of slop and hinders competitive advantage. 

Every new technology wave has brought its own gaggle of self-declared experts in the early days. They are focused much more on style rather than substance. Soundbites rather than strategy. Cynically, these self-declared experts are opportunists who are much more focused on their own short term career advancement than their employer’s long term growth. They are often the people peddling the indiscriminate and maximum use of new technologies, with complete disregard for value. 

There’s a long list of opportunists masquerading as experts in the recent technology waves around [social media, crypto, and the metaverse](https://michaelcarusi.com/ai-consultants-and-prompt-engineers-should-replace-their-own-jobs-with-ai-seriously/). AI is no different. The same opportunists have updated their resumes from “crypto strategist” to “AI expert”, selling the same snake oil with a new flashy label.

PwC found that [90 percent of financial services executives](https://cyprus-mail.com/2026/09/06/financial-firms-face-ai-workforce-gap-as-job-cuts-loom) believe employees using AI outside of governed systems are creating regulatory risk.

So, what should firms do instead of waiting for AI slop to pile up and business models potentially collapse?

Provide approved tools so employees do not go looking for their own solutions. 

Focus on business outcomes, not shiny stuff that looks good in a PowerPoint. 

Focus on foundational, high quality datasets that are controlled and governed. 

Implement a meaningful AI governance model. 

Require a named individual to review and take accountability for AI-generated content before it ever reaches a client or regulator.

**And perhaps more than anything else, when searching for AI expertise, firms need to find a way to expose the charlatans masquerading as experts.**

Although the scientific disciplines of AI and Machine Learning have been around for decades, AI as we are using it today is in its infancy. Anyone who claims to be an expert in AI should be treated with extreme scepticism.

The London Whale did not need a smarter model. The model was sound, but there was a point at which it should have been migrated off Excel and onto a structured database in a controlled production environment. 

Moreover, someone needed to stand up to the self-declared experts and tell them that their model is going to be implemented on a governed and scalable technology stack, not the spreadsheet slop pile. 

**In the coming years, firms are likely going to spend a lot of time and money backing themselves out of the AI slop piles that are being built today.**
